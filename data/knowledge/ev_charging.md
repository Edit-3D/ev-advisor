# EV Charging Fundamentals

## AC and DC charging

An electric vehicle battery stores direct-current energy. With AC charging, electricity from the grid reaches the vehicle and its onboard charger converts it to DC. The onboard charger therefore imposes a vehicle-side limit: connecting a car that accepts 11 kW AC to a higher-rated AC post does not make the car accept more than 11 kW. AC charging suits places where a vehicle remains parked, such as a home, workplace, or destination.

DC fast-charging equipment performs conversion outside the vehicle and supplies DC directly to the battery system. It can deliver much more power and is useful during travel stops. The vehicle must support the connector and charging protocol, and its own DC acceptance limit still applies. A charger's advertised maximum is not a promise of the power every car will receive.

## Charging levels, power, and energy

Charging terminology varies by region. The US Department of Energy describes Level 1 and Level 2 as AC charging: Level 1 uses a standard 120-volt outlet, while Level 2 normally uses a dedicated 208- or 240-volt circuit. These labels and voltages are US-specific. European drivers more commonly describe AC charging by power, voltage, and phase. Public networks typically combine destination AC charging with DC fast charging along travel corridors.

A kilowatt (kW) is power—the rate of energy transfer. A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is energy—the amount stored or delivered. Higher power can shorten a session, but only while the car can accept it. The useful charging choice depends on dwell time, energy needed, compatible hardware, and the electrical supply.

## Charging curves and speed limits

Actual power is constrained by the charger, cable, vehicle hardware, battery-management limits, and site supply. Battery temperature and state of charge also matter. A cold battery may accept less power while its thermal system warms it. During DC fast charging, power commonly tapers as state of charge rises because the battery-management system protects the cells.

This changing rate is the charging curve. Peak kW is only one point; average power across the useful session determines elapsed time. Arriving with a warm battery and lower state of charge can improve performance when the vehicle supports preconditioning. Power-sharing sites may divide capacity among plugs.

## Connectors and regional differences

Connector availability differs by vehicle, model year, and market. US guidance commonly discusses J1772 for AC and CCS, CHAdeMO, or J3400/NACS for DC-capable networks. Europe commonly uses Type 2 AC and CCS2 DC. Adapters do not automatically guarantee protocol, voltage, or network compatibility. Drivers should verify the vehicle inlet, charger connector, operator access, and regional standards before a trip.
